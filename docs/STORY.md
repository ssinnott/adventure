# The Dimming

*The one quest of DESIGN.md §9, told as a story: the chronicle a company might write of it,
afterwards. The design says what happens; this is how it should feel.*

---

## Act I · Ashes on the Road

*The Shelf and Thornmark, levels 1 to 10*

We were on the coast road when the light went out.

Everyone in Caldera has seen the Hearth every night of their life: a column of fire standing up out
of the middle of the sea, too bright to look at for long and as steady as the ground. That night it
stuttered. It went out and came back, and went out, and on the fourth time it stayed out long enough
for the stars to show over the water. Then it was there again, only the Hearth, and we stood in the
road feeling foolish for having been afraid.

In the morning the bells of Harrow were ringing for the Queen. Isaure had died in the night, and
nobody we asked could tell us of what.

Harrow had no heir that anyone agreed on, only a Regent-Warden. Lord Aumery Vask held the city
"until the succession is settled", and had the look of a man who meant to hold it a long while. He
had work for a new company. A farm called Ashcombe, south of the city, had gone quiet. He wanted to
know why, and he wanted anything we found there that was not a rat.

The cellar under Ashcombe was larger than the house above it, and older than the farm. On a wall on
the way down someone had scratched, in fresh marks, THE HEARTH IS A CAGE. At the bottom of its four
rings we found a woman in the grey of the Lanterns, dead against the wall, with a cracked survey
wand beside her and a note in her hand. *Not failing. CUT. The Grove Stone is next. Tell Vask
nothing.* Past her the floor had torn open. The Rift breathed heat like an oven door, and beside it
lay a fist of worked stone that hummed against our teeth: a shard of a Wardstone.

We were a hired company, and Vask was paying. We took him the wand. He turned it over for a long
moment and put it in his pocket, and if he knew what it was, his face did not say. He paid us three
hundred gold. We should have read the note twice.

Captain Hale kept the Warden post at the pass east, and he had a trouble he could not spare men for:
smugglers in the Greywater caves under the western cliffs. The caves smelled of salt and lamp oil.
Crates stamped with the Harrow customs seal lay broken open along the walls. In the middle cavern
was a tide pool, and things floated in it face down, in Harrow clothes, and one of them turned its
head. Chalked on the rock: SHIP DUE DARK OF THE MOON. CARGO GOES BELOW, NOT TO HARROW. Under the
caves ran dressed stone as old as Ashcombe's cellar, and a passage of cells, and we understood what
the cargo was. In the shrine at the bottom an Ashen Deacon was prising iron staples, one at a time,
out of a seam in the floor that glowed red. Over a blank stretch of wall, carved deep: THE HAND
OPENS WHAT THE HEARTH SHUTS.

We brought Hale the smugglers' ledger. He read it and went grey: names, dates, and a column headed
CARGO BELOW. "The Regent will want a copy," he said. We did not tell him about the note.

One of the names in that column we knew already. West of Harrow the Downs roll grey-green to the
sea, and in Gullwick, a village of nets and gulls, a woman had asked us to look for her daughter.
Wenna had gone down the coast in her father's boat in the autumn and not come back.

The Downs had a second grief in them. The Queen's barrow stood on the high ground, and someone had
opened it from outside. The grave goods were all there, gold and all. Only her signet ring was gone.
Out on the point beyond the Downs stands Harrow Light, and its keeper had kept a log the night she
died, as a man does who has nothing to watch but a light across the water. The Hearth had gone out
eleven times that night, not four. He had written down every one, and the gaps between them, and he
let us copy it.

The Wardens at the pass told us the forest beyond would kill us. They were nearly right. Thornmark's
trees are older than Harrow, and the Warden road under them had not been swept in weeks. There were
wolves the size of ponies, a ruined watchtower with something enormous denned in its base, a barrow
whose door-stone had been rolled aside from within. In a still lake a Lantern survey marker stood
half-sunk, its glass gone dark. In Thornhold, Elder Sylvane, in a robe the colour of bark, did not
rise when we came in. "The Grove Stone has been cut," she said. "Not failed: cut, by hands, with
tools. Vask will tell you the Stones are failing. Bring me the tool that cut ours, and we will both
know better."

In the Deepthorn, where the forest is oldest, one of the first elf-holds keeps a treaty two hundred
years old. It says that the elves' line and Harrow's were once the same line. Its seal is a mark we
had never seen before. We would see it again within the week, cut into iron.

The last stretch of road to the Grove had a chisel mark on every trunk at shoulder height. Under the
Grove the roots stood as thick as pillars, with steps cut into them, and by the stair lay an elf in
Thornhold green, a week dead, her hands burned to the wrist. Scratched beside an iron door: THE
STONE IS A LOCK. WE HAVE THE KEY. Beneath the roots ran three square rings of corridor too smooth to
have been dug, and at their heart stood the Grove Stone, a hand-span of it cut clean away, and the
air torn where the cut was. The Hand of Ash was waiting there. When the Hand was dead, the thing the
cut had let through came for us, the Warden of the Cut, and it was the hardest fight of our lives.
When it fell, the tear closed behind it, and on the floor where it died lay a book: the first volume
of the journal of the Meridian Company, who went down to map the Underdeep thirty years ago and
never came back.

Sylvane turned the chisel in her long fingers and read its runes. Underdeep work, she said, and a
maintenance mark, not a prayer. It was the seal on the Deepthorn treaty. "Whoever arms the Ashen
Hand can reach the Underdeep," she said. "Keep this from Vask."

That night, from Thornhold's walls, the Hearth looked a little steadier. The Stones were not
failing. They were being cut, with tools made under the world. And we had given Vask the wand.

## Act II · The Salt Road

*Saltreach, Wrackholm and Sunderwood, levels 10 to 16*

The coast road runs west along the Downs to the Ledge, where the Downs end in cliffs, and
switchbacks down to the Upper Water: reed country, where the Long Water moves slow and brown toward
the sea, and at night the barges go down it without lights. The ferryman at Reedholm had seen the
Tide Stone go by one night at midsummer, laid on a barge under sacking, "lit up from inside like a
lantern in a sack".

In the Delta its plinth stood empty on the salt, and the Drowned Temples had gone dark. The
Tidefolk's god had sung the tides in those temples for as long as there had been Tidefolk, their
last priestess told us. Now it only counted. She said the number for us. It meant nothing to her, or
to us, and she said it again.

Saltmouth is the free port at the end of everything, where the Salt Compact sells what Caldera needs
and does not ask what for. Its dockmaster, Jory Tallis, was one of the three who claimed Harrow's
empty throne, and he had the only navy to back his claim. His lineage was very good. It was too
good: a crew record, he called it, written in a script like the chisel's runes. Somebody had copied
it for him from something below.

Word came down the coast while we were there. Hale's copy of the ledger had reached the Regent. Hale
himself had not been seen since, and his post at the pass was held now by men we did not know.

The Compact's own boat took us out to Wrackholm, the smugglers' isle, for a price we did not haggle
over. Past Smugglers' Cove the Tide Ship rode low at anchor. Its hold was packed with Wardstone
shards in straw, and with people chained in rows, bound for a dead-drop in the Underdeep. The
Compact did not give the orders for that cargo. The orders came up from below; the Compact did as it
was told, and was paid. Carved into a beam over the chained rows, in the Hand's square capitals:
EVERY SHARD IS A STEP.

The Tide Stone was in the forward hold, glowing through its sacking. We brought it home. The night
it went back on its plinth, the Hearth burned steadier than on any night since the Queen died, and
the priestess wept in the door of her temple, because her god had stopped counting and begun, very
faintly, to sing.

East, then, back through Thornmark and out along the east road into the Eaves, to the Sunder. A Rift
had split a whole wood there. The trees at its edge had turned to crystal, and the forest floor fell
away into a gorge you could not see across in the rain. We climbed down it. At the bottom, under the
soil and the rock, stood a wall: flat, seamless, too smooth to be stone, going down further than our
light would reach. Over the gorge, by a rope bridge, stands Lantern Watch, and the Lanterns there
were already arguing about that wall. Some wanted to mend the Stones. Some wanted to read them. It
was the first time we heard a Lantern use the word *machine*.

We had brought the Tide Ship's log with us, and at Lantern Watch it was read. Every cargo in it, the
shards and the people, had gone below under the Harrow customs seal we had seen on the crates at
Greywater, and every one was countersigned by the Regent. Vask was the hand behind the Hand. He had
read the Underdeep's writing and believed it: that the Hearth is a cage, and there is a world beyond
the sky. He was not wrong about the facts. He was wrong about what "beyond" means.

Harrow had changed when we came home. There were Wardens on the walls and a curfew bell, and the
Lantern chapel was shut. It would change again every time we came back, and never for the better.

## Act III · The Deep Script

*The Kilns, Cairnmoor and Rimewater, levels 16 to 22*

In the Iron Fells the dwarves carve their scripture over every door, and read it aloud at their
forges the way Harrow says its prayers. At Anvilhall we learned that the Anvil Stone was being cut
too, and not by the Hand. The dwarves were cutting it themselves and selling the shards down the
coast to the Compact, who sold them below. "The mountain has to eat," the thane told us. We could
buy the Stone back or take it. Whichever we did, he did not forgive us, and that night the Hearth
stood a little brighter over the sea.

We learned to read Kiln-script that winter, a word at a time, and every inscription we read opened
more of the Kilns to us. The holiest verse in Anvilhall, carved over the great forge, is THE FIRE IS
KEPT BELOW AND NOT ABOVE. Read the old way, word for word, it is a warning painted on a boiler. The
lowest door of the Deep Mines says CREW ONLY. Every scripture in the Kilns was a sign on a door. The
Kilns stopped being a holy place for us that winter, and became what they were: a machine that had
outlived everyone who knew how to run it.

The Deep Mines had broken through into the service layer. Past the last of the dwarves' timbering
the rock simply ended, and there was corridor: clean, lit from nowhere, humming, the air tasting of
nothing at all. The dust on its floor was full of footprints, bare and shod, walking down in a line.
The cargo had come this way.

Cairnmoor is heather and wind and stones. On High Moor there is a ring of stones older than the
Wardstones, dead for centuries, with the cairns of the Cairnfield all around it. We slept inside the
ring one night, and in the dark something spoke in it, very old and very faint, like a man talking
in his sleep. It did not sing, and it did not count. It asked the crew to report.

At Rime Lodge, on the shore of Longmere, people were coming up through the ice. They came at night,
a few at a time, blue-lipped and frostbitten, out of a hole in the lake that the lodge-keepers had
learned to keep open. They had been taken below from all over Caldera. Below, they said, there were
doors, and the doors opened for some of them and not for others. The ones the doors did not know
were kept to carry. The ones the doors knew were taken on, further down. One of those had got away,
and climbed up the way the water goes, and brought the others with her. It was Wenna of Gullwick.

The way they came up was the way down. Under Coldmere's frozen lake there is a sealed bay, and we
went into it with Wenna's hand on the door. Inside were long glass cradles in rows, frosted over,
and in the cradles lay sleepers who had never woken: a dwarf with our dwarf's jaw, an elf with our
elf's long hands, gnomes, Tidefolk, orcblood, humans. Every people of Caldera, laid out in rows like
cargo, four hundred years asleep. Scratched across the inside of the door, in the Hand's capitals:
THE BLOOD OPENS THE DOOR.

The Stones were machines. And the people of Caldera, all of us, had come here with them.

## Act IV · Beyond the Sky

*The Whitespine, Ashfall and the Wold, levels 22 to 28*

South of Coldmere the road climbs into the Whitespine, and on the second day we heard the
monastery's bells come across the snow. They rang eleven times, with gaps between, and then again:
the same eleven, the same gaps. We had the keeper's log from Harrow Light with us. It was the night
the Queen died, rung over and over.

The monks of Monks' Vale walked wrong and spoke Kiln-script among themselves, and they were not
monks. The monks had died a long time ago. Something had gone on keeping the monastery for them,
wearing their robes, keeping their hours, guarding the Peak Stone, which was whole and well. It was
the Custodian's own hands, keeping a heartbeat for a patient nobody else was watching. We fought our
way up through that house floor by floor, and at the top, in the bell-chamber, found the bells
ringing the Hearth's pulse: rung by a machine that had been told to ring them and never told to
stop.

From the tip of Sheer Point the Hearth is close enough to feel on your face. Below the point the
Ashen Hand were building their crossing: a causeway of cut stone running out across the water toward
the light, and every stone in it a Wardstone shard. EVERY SHARD IS A STEP. On the first stone they
had painted THE SKY IS A LID. We watched their boats go out along it at dusk, and in the last of
them, between two of the Hand, sat Wenna.

Over the High Spine the Giants' Stair goes down the far side of the range, past the giants who gave
it its name, into Ashfall. Cinderport stands on black sand under cliffs hung with vines, with Fire
Mountain smoking over everything. The Wold Riders come down to its market from the steppe, and we
rode back up onto the Wold with them. Their eldest told us the oldest story on the far side of the
sea. Long ago, in the west, a door opened in the sky. Something rose toward it on a pillar of fire,
and fell, and the land where it fell burned to glass. The Riders' first mothers walked out of that
fire. The Riders keep the way into the Glass, and let no one in. "What lies in the Glass tried to
leave," she said. "The sky opened for it. Go and look at what that cost, if you like. Then come and
tell me you still want it open."

Fire Mountain breathes. Its vents are the Underdeep's exhaust, and we went down them for the parts
the Ember Stone had always lacked, through iron corridors hot enough to blister, to the Meridian
Company's last camp. Their cartographer was still there, thirty years down, very old. His name was
Oriel Fane. "You took your time," he said. He did not show us his map; that, he said, was for those
who had followed every step his company took. But he showed us the window.

Beyond it was night, and in the night, a long way below, lay a world: blue at its edge, marbled with
cloud, turning. Between us and it curved the outside of everything we had ever known.

"You see," Fane said, "why we didn't come back."

The Ember Stone had never been finished. It stood half-built in the Ember Waste, in a field of
cinders, and we finished it with the parts we had carried up from below. When it lit, the Underdeep
noticed. Every door below opened at once, and things came up out of them that had been waiting four
hundred years for someone to touch the Stones. When it was over, the Hearth burned steadier than it
had in our lifetime.

Caldera was a vessel. The sky was a lid. And the Hand had Wenna, and a road of stolen stones to the
Hearth.

## Act V · The Hearth

*Hearth Isle, the Underdeep and the Core, levels 28 to 32*

The last crossing leaves from Cinderport. Close to, the Hearth is not a fire at all. It is a column
of light standing out of the sea into the roof of the sky, humming so low you feel it in your teeth,
the way the shard under Ashcombe hummed. On its isle stands a temple, built by people who thought it
was a god, and under the temple the Underdeep goes down.

The Hand had come in before us, along their causeway and down the service ways, and every door on
their road had opened for them, because they had Wenna's hand to open it. We found their dead on the
stairs; the Underdeep had not liked them. The core's door would not open for them at all. It opened
for us, because we had put the Stones back, and the Custodian knew who had healed it. Across the
door the Hand had carved the last line of their creed: PUT OUT THE LIGHT, AND THE DOOR WILL OPEN.

Vask was inside, at the heart of the Hearth, halfway through the shutdown. The Queen's signet was on
his finger, and two of the Hand held Wenna's palm flat against a panel of glass that had waited four
hundred years for a hand like hers. Overhead, through the roof, we could see the light going out.

"Four hundred years in a jar," he said. "They painted the lid blue and called it heaven, and you
have spent your lives mapping the inside of the jar. You have read the walls by now, as I have.
There is a world beyond the sky, and I am opening the door."

He was not wrong about the facts.

When the Hand was broken and Vask was on his knees, the Custodian spoke. The Stones had made it
whole, and for the first time every voice we had heard on the road was one voice: the singing and
the counting, the Green Man and the bells, the old thing in the ring on the moor that had asked the
crew to report.

*The voyage is over. It has been over for four hundred years. The vessel is in orbit around the
world it was sent to. Arrival was never triggered because the crew who could trigger it forgot they
were crew.*

Wenna took the signet from Vask's hand. She looked at it, and then at us. "You walked all of it,"
she said. "Every road. You choose."

### The last page

This chronicle ends three ways, and every company writes one of them.

**Reignite.** We gave the Hearth back its fire. It stood up out of the sea brighter than anyone
living had seen it, and the Rifts closed all across Caldera, one by one, like doors in a house at
night. The sky stayed blue. The world we had spent our lives mapping was the world we kept, and the
Wardens and the Lanterns called it a victory, and it was. Vask lived to stand trial in Harrow, and
never once looked up.

**Arrive.** We opened the hull. The Rifts stopped for good, because there was nothing left for them
to hold out. The Lanterns split over it and never healed; the Cartographers and the Compact drank to
it for a week. We walked up out of the Underdeep into daylight that was the wrong colour, under a
sky that went up and did not stop, and felt real wind. Vask walked out ahead of us, and was the
first of us to understand what "beyond" meant.

**The third.** Only a company that has followed the Meridian journals to the end knows this. Oriel
Fane's map shows what the Custodian would not say: the vessel is not in orbit. It never arrived. The
voyage is not over; it stalled. So we did the one thing nobody had asked of us, and made it finish.
The Hearth burned harder than it had ever burned, and somewhere below us the engines woke. We will
not see the landing. Our grandchildren will, if we tell them the truth, and we have written it down
here so that we can.

## Off the road

Two places in this chronicle were never on the road, and did not need us. We went anyway.

Behind the Wold, past the Riders who let no one in, lies the Glass: a desert of fused glass where
the thing that tried to leave came down. A mast stands up out of the middle of it, and that is the
Buried Tower, the top of a lander that tried to arrive, sunk in the glass it made, its decks running
down and down into the ground it melted. What waits in it is harder than anything under the Hearth.

In Rimewater's far corner the glacier comes down from the rim of the world to Glacier Foot, and the
Ice Caves open under it. At the glacier's head, where the ice meets the rim, the sky comes down to
meet the ice. It is the one place in Caldera where a company can climb into the sky: into the Vault,
the inside of the lid, where the lights that make the sun and the moon hang in the dark. You can see
its way in from the Longmere road long before you are fit to take it. We looked at it every day of
the winter we spent at Rime Lodge.

## What the telling added

Wenna of Gullwick, Hale's fate, the eleven flickers, the creed's later lines, what the gods say,
Kiln-script read plainly, the causeway of shards, the two ways into the core, the sleepers as every
people of Caldera, the Vault's lights, and the names Jory Tallis and Oriel Fane all began in this
