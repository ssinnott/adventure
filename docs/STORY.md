# The Dimming

*The one quest of DESIGN.md §9, told as a story, and written to be heard. Wren, the premade
company's elf ranger, tells it; the short chapters headed Wenna are hers. The design says what
happens; this is how it should feel.*

---

## Act One: Ashes on the Road

### Wren

The night the light went out, we were camped above Gullwick, close enough to hear the sea. There
were six of us, a company so new our charter still smelled of wax, and I was the one who drew the
maps.

I remember the dogs first. Every dog in the village started barking at once, and when I looked up,
the Hearth was gone. Not dim. Gone. For as long as it takes to say a prayer, there was nothing over
the water but dark. Then it came back. Then it went again.

By the time we got down to the shore, half the village was standing in the surf with lanterns. The
fishermen steer home by the Hearth at night. It's the only lighthouse they've ever needed, and there
were boats still out. The old keeper from the lighthouse on the point stood at the water's edge,
counting under his breath, the way you count between lightning and thunder.

"Eleven," he said, when it was over. "It went out eleven times."

Nobody asked him why that mattered. It turned out to matter a great deal.

One of the boats never came in. The woman who had been waiting for it didn't cry. She walked
straight up to the biggest of us and took Bram by the sleeve. There was a knot tied into the rope at
her belt, a loop inside a loop, the same mark I'd seen painted on the boats along the beach.

"You're a company," she said. "You find things. Find my girl. Her name is Wenna."

Bram said we would. None of us knew yet that finding her, and finding out what had happened to the
light, would turn out to be the same job.

### Wenna

Wenna was halfway home when the light went out.

She had been steering by it all evening, the way her father taught her. Keep the Hearth over the
left oar, and the cliffs will never find you. Then there was no Hearth. The sea went black from one
edge of the world to the other. Her father stopped rowing, and neither of them spoke.

When the light came back, they were not alone. Another boat lay close off their stern, showing no
lantern, full of men who had been waiting in the dark for exactly this.

They took the catch, and then the boat. They took Wenna last, and they were careful about it, the
way you are careful with something that has already been paid for.

Days later, far below the ground, a man with ash-grey hands pressed her palm flat against a door. It
was cold, and smooth, and older than anything she had ever touched.

The door opened.

For the first time, the man with the grey hands smiled. "Well," he said. "There you are."

### Wren

In the morning, the bells of Harrow were ringing. The Queen had died in the night, and nobody could
tell us of what.

With the Queen dead, the city belonged, for now, to her Regent, Lord Vask. He was a tall man with a
quiet voice, who said he would hold Harrow "until the succession is settled", and looked like
someone who meant to hold it for a very long time. We went to him because we had a promise to keep
and no money to keep it with.

He had work for us. A farm called Ashcombe had gone quiet, and he wanted to know why.

The cellar under Ashcombe went down much further than any farmer would dig. On the way down, someone
had scratched five words into the old stone. "The Hearth is a cage." At the bottom lay a woman in
the grey robes of the Lanterns, the order that looks after the great standing Stones. She was dead,
with a cracked wand beside her and a note in her hand. Cassian read it out, because Cassian reads
everything.

"Not failing. Cut. The Grove Stone is next. Tell Vask nothing."

Past her, the floor had torn open into a glowing wound the size of a door, breathing heat like an
oven, and something came up out of it at us. When it was dead, we found a lump of carved stone lying
by the tear. It hummed so hard you could feel it in your teeth. A piece of one of the great Stones.

We argued about that note all the way back to Harrow. Cassian wanted to keep the wand. Bram said a
job is a job. In the end we were hungry, and Vask was paying, and we handed it over. He turned the
wand over in his long fingers, and put it in his pocket, and if he knew what it was, his face didn't
say.

Three days later, someone broke open the Queen's tomb and took her signet ring. Nothing else. Not
the gold, not the jewels. Just the ring. We didn't think much about it at the time.

We should have read the note twice.

* * *

Captain Hale held the Warden post on the road east, and Bram had served under him once, years
before. Hale had smugglers in the sea caves at Greywater, and no men to spare, and a list of missing
fishing boats as long as his arm. That was enough for us. If anyone knew where a stolen boat went,
it would be smugglers.

The caves stank of salt and lamp oil. Crates were stacked along the walls, stamped with the Harrow
customs seal, which smugglers are not supposed to have. In the middle cavern was a tide pool, and
things floated in it face down, and one of them turned its head.

Under the caves the rock gave way to old dressed stone, and a passage of cells. The cells were
empty, but the straw in them was fresh. We were a few days too late.

At the very bottom, in a little shrine, a priest of the Ashen Hand was prying iron staples, one at a
time, out of a crack in the floor that glowed red. Above him someone had carved, "The Hand opens
what the Hearth shuts." We didn't understand that. We understood him well enough, and when he was
dead, we took his ledger back to Hale.

Hale read it by his fire, and went grey. It was a list of names and dates, with a column headed
"cargo below". Halfway down, in a neat clerk's hand, it said, "Wenna, of Gullwick."

"The Regent will want a copy of this," Hale said.

We didn't tell him about the note. And the note said the Grove Stone was next. So we went east, to
the old forest, to get to the Grove before whoever was cutting the Stones.

* * *

The Wardens at the pass told us the forest would kill us. They were nearly right. Thornmark's trees
are older than Harrow, and there are wolves under them the size of ponies.

The elves of Thornmark are my people, more or less. Their elder, Sylvane, didn't rise when we came
in. "The Grove Stone has been cut," she said. "Not failed. Cut, by hands, with tools. Vask will tell
you the Stones are failing. Bring me the tool that cut ours, and we will both know better."

The cutters had beaten us to it. Under the Grove, down stairs carved into roots as thick as pillars,
the Stone stood in the dark with a hand's width of it sliced clean away. The air in the cut was torn
open, just like the floor at Ashcombe. The leader of the Ashen Hand was waiting beside it, with the
chisel still in his belt. And when he was dead, the thing that had come through the tear came for
us. It was the worst fight of our lives.

When it finally fell, the tear sealed shut behind it. Where it died, there was a book. A journal,
water-stained and swollen, from a company of explorers who had gone down under the world thirty
years before and never come back. On its last page, in a shaking hand, someone had written, "The
heart opens for whoever makes it whole."

We brought Sylvane the chisel. She turned it over in her fingers for a long time. The marks on it
weren't prayers, she said. They were the kind of marks a smith puts on a tool, to say what it's for.
And she had seen them once before, on the seal of an old treaty her people keep, which says that the
elves' royal line and Harrow's were once the same.

"Whoever armed these people can reach the world beneath this one," she said. "Keep this from Vask."

That night, from the elves' walls, the Hearth looked a little steadier. The Stones weren't failing.
Somebody was cutting them, with tools made under the world. And we had given Vask the wand.

## Act Two: The Salt Road

### Wren

We went west because of the ledger. Every name in that column had gone out through the salt coast,
on the same ship.

And Maren hadn't been home to the salt coast in ten years. Maren is our healer. She's Tidefolk, one
of the sea people of the delta, who swim like otters and sing to a god in drowned temples. On the
road down she sang us their tide-songs. By the time we reached the coast, she'd stopped.

The temples were dark. Their god, the priestess told us, used to sing the tides. Now it only
counted. She said the number for us, again and again, and it meant nothing to any of us. The great
Stone of the salt flats, the Tide Stone, had been stolen at midsummer and carried off down the river
on a barge, glowing through its sacking like a lantern in a sack.

Maren went into the temple alone. When she came out, her face was set. "We're getting it back," she
said. Nobody argued.

That was where Bram got his news, too. A Warden coming down the coast road told him that Hale's copy
of the ledger had reached the Regent, and that Hale himself hadn't been seen since. His post was
held now by men nobody knew. Bram didn't say a word for the rest of that day.

The ship we wanted was anchored off the smugglers' island, and the only way out there was on a
smugglers' boat. Ottilie got us one. Ottilie is a gnome, and our thief, and she used to run with the
smugglers of the salt coast, a long time ago. She went into a harbour tavern alone, and came out an
hour later with a boat, and a captain who wouldn't look her in the eye.

* * *

The ship rode low at anchor under the island's cliffs, and we went over its side at night.

The hold held two kinds of cargo. There were shards of the great Stones, packed in straw like eggs.
And there were people, chained in rows. Fishermen and farmers and children, bound for somewhere
below the world. The smugglers weren't giving the orders. The orders came up from below, and the
smugglers did what they were told, and got paid. Carved into a beam above the chained rows were four
words. "Every shard is a step."

We broke the chains. Wenna wasn't there. But Hale was. Bram found his old captain in the last row,
thin and filthy and still giving orders. "The Regent got his copy," Hale said, when Bram had him on
his feet. "He came for me that same night."

The ship's papers were there too. They said the girl from Gullwick had been "delivered below, as
instructed", months before, through the dwarves' deepest mine. So she was alive. She was still
alive.

The Tide Stone was in the forward hold, glowing through its sacking. It took all six of us to carry
it. The night we set it back on its salt flat, the Hearth burned steadier than it had since the
Queen died. And in the dark temple behind us, something began, very faintly, to sing. Maren sat down
on the temple steps and cried like a child. None of us looked at her, because we were all doing the
same.

### Wenna

Below the world, there were no nights.

There were only corridors, clean and humming and lit from nowhere, and doors, and the men with
ash-grey hands, who walked Wenna from one door to the next. Every door was the same. Smooth, cold,
older than anything. Every door opened when they pressed her palm against it. They didn't open for
the grey-handed men. They didn't open for the other prisoners, who were put to carrying stone in the
dark. Only for her.

And every time a door opened, something in the walls spoke. It was very soft, like someone talking
in their sleep in the next room. It always said the same word, and Wenna didn't know what it meant.

"Captain?"

So she began to leave marks. Her family's knot, a loop inside a loop, the one her mother tied into
every net. She scratched it into the frame of every door she opened, with a nail she'd worked loose
from a crate. She didn't know who would ever see it. It just seemed wrong to walk through all those
doors, and leave no sign that a girl from Gullwick had been there.

### Wren

East of the elves' forest, the ground has split open. A Rift tore a whole wood in half there. The
trees along its edges have turned to glass, and the valley floor falls away into a gorge so deep you
can't see the bottom in the rain.

We climbed down it, because I make maps, and a map with a hole in it is an insult. At the bottom,
under the soil and the rock, there was a wall. Flat and seamless, smoother than any stone, going
down further than our light could reach. I put my hand on it. It was warm.

The Lanterns keep a watchtower on the far side of the gorge, and there, they read the smugglers'
papers for us. Every cargo, the shards and the people, had gone below under the Harrow customs seal,
the same seal as on the crates in the caves. And every page was countersigned by the Regent.

Vask came himself, the next morning. He rode up to the watchtower with two Wardens and no fear at
all, and asked for us by name.

"You've read the papers," he said. "Good. Then you know half of what I know."

He told us the rest, standing in the rain. That the world is a cage, and the Hearth is its lock.
That beyond the sky there's somewhere else, somewhere real, and he meant to open the door. "Help
me," he said. "You've touched that wall. You know I'm right."

He wasn't wrong about the wall. We told him no anyway. He didn't seem surprised.

"Then stay out of my way," he said. "I don't need you. I have the girl."

When we got back to Harrow, there were Wardens on the walls, and a curfew bell, and the Lanterns'
chapel was boarded shut. At the gate, a sergeant stepped in front of Idris. Idris is our paladin,
and orcblood, and he's been spat at in better cities than Harrow.

"No orcblood past the gate," the sergeant said. "Regent's orders."

Idris just nodded, as if he'd been expecting it all his life. We turned around and walked away with
him, all of us. Harrow wasn't our city any more.

## Act Three: The Deep Script

### Wren

The ship's papers said the cargo had gone below through the dwarves' deepest mine. So we went to the
dwarf country.

In the dwarf halls, the scripture is carved over every door, and the dwarves read it aloud at their
forges the way other people pray. Cassian decided to learn it. He sat up every night that winter
with a lexicon and a candle, and slowly, word by word, he taught himself to read it the way it was
first written.

The holiest verse in the great hall is carved above the forge where the dwarves make their kings'
crowns. It says, "The fire is kept below, and not above." Cassian read it the old way, word by word.
Then he went very quiet.

"It's a warning," he said. "The kind you paint on a boiler."

It was all like that. Every holy word in the dwarf halls was a sign on a door. And the dwarves had
been cutting their own great Stone for years, and selling the pieces to the smugglers, who sold them
below. "The mountain has to eat," their thane told us. We could buy the Stone back, or take it.
Either way, he never forgave us. That night, the Hearth stood a little brighter over the sea.

At the bottom of the deepest mine, the dwarves' tunnel simply ended, and a clean, humming corridor
began. The door at the end was marked in the old script. Cassian read it. "Crew only." It wouldn't
open for us. But in the dust in front of it were hundreds of footprints, all walking down, in a
line. And scratched into the frame, at about the height of a girl's shoulder, was a loop inside a
loop.

Bram touched it with one finger. "That's her," he said. "That's the knot off her mother's rope."

We couldn't follow her through that door. But the dwarves said the corridors ran south under the
world, toward the lakes beneath the glacier. So that's where we went.

* * *

We crossed the moor in the snow. There's a ring of old stones up there, older than the great Stones,
dead for centuries, and we slept inside it one night because there was nowhere else out of the wind.
In the dark, something spoke inside the ring. It was very old, and very faint, like a man talking in
his sleep.

"Crew," it said. "Report."

Nobody slept after that.

At the lodge on the shore of the long lake, people were coming up through the ice. They came at
night, a few at a time, blue-lipped and frostbitten, out of a hole the lodge-keepers kept open with
fire. They'd been taken below from all over the world. Down there, they said, there were doors, and
the doors opened for some people and not for others.

The last one out, on the fourth night, was a girl of about fifteen, with a nail in her fist and her
hair frozen to her face. She had led every one of the others up, climbing the way the water runs.
She looked at the six of us, standing there in the firelight with our weapons and our maps.

"Are you the ones my mother sent?" she said.

Bram laughed, and then he cried, and then he picked her up, frozen hair and all.

* * *

Wenna wouldn't go home. Not yet. "There are two hundred more of us down there," she said, "carrying
stone in the dark. I'm going back for them. The doors know me."

So she took us down the way she'd come up. Under the frozen lake there was a sealed door. She laid
her palm on it, and it opened, and a soft voice in the wall said, "Captain?" She flinched. Then we
went in.

Inside were rows of long glass beds, frosted over, and in them lay people. Sleepers who had never
woken. There was an orcblood man with Idris's heavy brow, and a Tidefolk woman who could have been
Maren's grandmother. There was a gnome no bigger than Ottilie, and dwarves, and humans, and an elf
with my own long hands. Every people in the world was there, laid out in rows like cargo, asleep for
four hundred years. On the inside of the door, someone had scratched, "The blood opens the door."

Cassian wiped the frost off one of the lids and stood there for a long time. "We didn't grow here,"
he said at last. "We were brought."

The Stones were machines. And every one of us, every people in the world, had come here with them,
in rows, asleep.

## Act Four: Beyond the Sky

### Wren

The prisoners Wenna had left behind, she said, were being marched south, toward the mountains and
the sea. So we went south.

There's a monastery high in those mountains, and on our second day climbing toward it, we heard its
bells come across the snow. They rang eleven times, with gaps between. And then again. The same
eleven, the same gaps. I thought of the old lighthouse keeper standing in the surf at Gullwick,
counting. The bells were ringing the night the Queen died, over and over.

The monks walked wrong. They spoke the dwarves' old script to each other, and when Idris struck one,
it didn't bleed. The monks had died a long time ago. Something had gone on keeping the monastery for
them. Wearing their robes. Keeping their hours. Ringing their bells, because it had been told to,
long ago, and no one had ever told it to stop. It was the voice from the moor, the voice in the
walls, and it was keeping a heartbeat for a patient nobody else was watching.

Beyond the mountains, a long finger of land runs out into the sea toward the Hearth, so close you
can feel its heat on your face. The Ashen Hand were building a road out there. A causeway across the
water, built of cut stone, and every stone in it was a stolen shard. Every shard is a step.

They came for Wenna that night. We never saw them. We woke to Maren shouting and an empty bedroll,
and down on the causeway, in the moonlight, a boat going out along the stones toward the Hearth. On
the first stone of the causeway, scratched fresh, at the height of a girl's shoulder, was a loop
inside a loop.

We couldn't follow them across the water. Not without being cut to pieces. We sat on the shore that
night, the six of us, and nobody spoke, until Cassian took out the explorers' journal we'd found
under the Grove and read its last line again.

"The heart opens for whoever makes it whole."

"There's one Stone left," he said. "On the far side of the sea. The one that was never finished."

### Wenna

The grey-handed men made her open every door on the road to the Hearth. There were more of them now
than ever, and they walked faster, and they had stopped talking to her.

She scratched her knot into every frame.

The voice in the walls was louder here, and clearer. It didn't say "Captain?" any more, as if it
were asking. It said "Captain," every time, the way you'd greet someone you'd been waiting for.

Wenna had worked it out by then. The doors weren't opening for her. They were opening for someone
she came from. A great-grandmother of a great-grandmother, who had stood at a wheel somewhere and
given orders, back before anyone forgot what the world was. The grey-handed men didn't want Wenna.
They wanted a dead woman's hand.

"I'm not anyone's key," she told the wall, very quietly, at the last door.

It opened anyway.

### Wren

We went over the mountains by the stair the giants use, and down the far side into the land of ash.
It's black sand there, and hanging vines, and a mountain that smokes over everything.

The horse people of the steppe come down to the port there to trade, and their eldest told us the
oldest story on that side of the sea. Long ago, she said, a door opened in the sky. Something rose
toward it on a pillar of fire, and fell, and the land where it fell burned to glass. "What lies in
the glass tried to leave," she said. "The sky opened for it. Remember what that cost, if anyone ever
offers to open it for you."

The last unfinished Stone stood on a field of cinders, half-built, and the parts it lacked could
only be found below. So we went down into the smoking mountain, through vents like chimneys and iron
corridors hot enough to blister, until we found a camp. Tents, and a cooking fire, and an old man
sitting beside it as if he'd been expecting us. He was the last of the explorers from the journal,
thirty years down.

"You took your time," he said.

He had something to show us. I'd come a long way with my maps by then. I had drawn the forest and
the salt coast, the dwarf halls and the lakes under the ice, and I'd always meant, one day, to draw
the edge of the world. The old man took us to a window.

On the other side, it was night. And down in the night, a long way below, there was a world. Blue at
its edges, marbled with cloud, turning slowly in the dark. Between us and it curved the outside of
everything we had ever known.

I had found the edge of the world. It was a wall, and we were on the inside of it.

"You see," the old man said, "why we didn't come back."

We carried the parts up, and we finished the Stone. When it lit, every door below the world opened
at once, and things came up out of them that had been waiting four hundred years for someone to
touch the Stones. When it was over, the Hearth burned steadier than it had in all our lives. And
somewhere under it, Wenna was opening doors for the people who wanted to put it out.

## Act Five: The Hearth

### Wren

Up close, the Hearth isn't a fire at all. It's a column of light, standing up out of the sea into
the roof of the sky, and it hums so deep you feel it in your teeth. It was the same hum as that lump
of carved stone under the farm at Ashcombe, right at the very beginning.

There's a temple on its island, built by people who thought it was a god. And under the temple, the
world goes down.

We didn't need the doors to open for us. Wenna had left us a trail. A loop inside a loop, scratched
on every frame, all the way down. We found the Ashen Hand's dead on the stairs, where the things
that live below had caught up with them. And at the bottom, we found the last door, the door to the
heart of the Hearth. It didn't wait for anyone's palm. It opened as we came near, the way a door
opens for someone it knows. We had made the Stones whole, and the heart opens for whoever makes it
whole.

Carved across it were the Ashen Hand's last words. "Put out the light, and the door will open."

* * *

Vask was inside, at the heart of it all, halfway through putting out the light. The Queen's signet
ring was on his finger. Two of his grey-handed men were holding Wenna's palm flat against a panel of
glass, a panel that had waited four hundred years for a hand like hers. Overhead, through the roof,
we could see the light going out.

"Four hundred years in a jar," Vask said. "They painted the lid blue and called it heaven." He
looked straight at me. "And you've spent your whole life mapping the inside of the jar. I'm opening
the door."

He wasn't wrong about the facts. That's the worst of it. He never was.

It was a hard fight, and I won't pretend it was a clean one. When it was over, Vask was on his
knees, and his grey-handed men were gone, and Wenna was standing in the middle of the room, rubbing
her wrist.

Then the voice spoke. The Stones had made it whole, and for the first time, every voice we'd heard
on the road was one voice. The drowned god's song, and the counting, and the monastery bells, and
the old thing in the stone ring on the moor, asking the crew to report.

"The voyage is over," it said. "It has been over for four hundred years. The vessel is in orbit
around the world it was sent to. Arrival was never triggered, because the crew who could trigger it
forgot they were crew."

Wenna walked over to Vask and pulled the Queen's ring off his finger. She looked at it for a long
moment. Then she looked at the six of us, and she put the ring in my hand.

"I'm not anyone's key," she said. "You walked every road of it. You choose."

### The last page

This is where the chronicle splits. I know which way we chose. Every company that walks this road
chooses its own.

If you reignite the Hearth, it stands up out of the sea brighter than anyone living has seen it, and
the Rifts close all across the world, one by one, like doors in a house at night. The sky stays
blue. The world you spent your life mapping is the world you keep. Vask stands trial in Harrow, and
never once looks up.

If you choose to arrive, you open the hull. The Rifts stop for good, because there's nothing left
for them to hold out. You walk up out of the dark into daylight that's the wrong colour, under a sky
that goes up and doesn't stop, and for the first time in your life, you feel real wind. Vask walks
out ahead of you, and he's the first to understand what "beyond" really means.

And if you've found every one of the lost explorers' journals, you know the one thing the voice
wouldn't say. The ship isn't in orbit. It never arrived. The voyage didn't end. It stalled. So you
do the one thing nobody asked you to do, and you make it finish. The Hearth burns harder than it has
ever burned, and somewhere far below, engines wake. You won't see the landing. Your grandchildren
will, if you tell them the truth.

But one thing is the same on every page. In the spring, we walked Wenna home to Gullwick, along the
coast road. Harrow's gate stood open when we passed, and nobody said a word to Idris. And her mother
was standing on the shore, right where we'd left her, as if she'd never moved.

This time, she cried.

## Notes for the design

This telling is the second draft. The first was a tour of every zone, and heard aloud it could not
be followed, so this one follows a few rules instead: one narrator and one second voice, a scene
only where it causes the next, no more than a dozen names a listener must hold, every promise the
opening makes paid off by the end, and sentences written for the ear. The places it passes without
naming, and the two reach areas it leaves out, are all still in DESIGN.md.

It adds these to the design, to take in or leave:

- Wren, the premade elf ranger, tells it, and each of the other five has a moment of their own: Bram
  with Hale, Maren with the drowned god, Ottilie with the smugglers' boat, Idris at Harrow's gate,
  Cassian with the dwarves' script.
- The Hearth is the fishermen's lighthouse, and Wenna's boat is taken in the dark the night it goes
  out. The lighthouse keeper counts the eleven from the shore.
- Wenna's family knot, a loop inside a loop, is her trail through the doors, and the company's way
  down to the core.
- The voice in the walls calls Wenna "Captain".
- Hale is found chained in the Tide Ship's hold.
- Vask comes to the Lanterns' watchtower to recruit the company: "I don't need you. I have the
  girl."
- Harrow turns Idris away at its gate.
- The explorers' journal ends "The heart opens for whoever makes it whole", which is why the company
  finishes the last Stone.
- Wenna goes back below for the others, and the Ashen Hand take her from the shore by the causeway.
- Wenna puts the Queen's ring in Wren's hand, and in every ending the company walks her home to
  Gullwick.

Where this and DESIGN.md disagree, the design wins.
